# How to Support Grieving Children After the Death of a Family Member

While the adults in a family are working through funeral arrangements, paperwork, and their own grief, children in the household are processing the same loss — often without the words or context to make sense of it. Here's practical, age-informed guidance for supporting them through it.

## Start with honest, simple language

It's a natural instinct to soften the news, but euphemisms like "went to sleep" or "went away" can genuinely confuse young children and, in some cases, create fear around ordinary sleep or travel. Clear, simple, honest language tends to serve children better:

> "Grandpa's body stopped working, and he died. That means we won't see him anymore, and it's really sad. It's okay to feel sad, and it's okay to ask me anything about it."

Use the words "died" and "death" directly rather than avoiding them — children pick up on avoidance, and it can signal that the topic is too frightening or shameful to discuss openly, which tends to make things harder, not easier.

## What to expect by age

This is generally consistent with guidance from child bereavement organizations like the [National Alliance for Children's Grief](https://nacg.org/):

**Young children (roughly 2–6):** Death is often understood as temporary or reversible at this age — a child might ask when the person is coming back, more than once, even after being told. This isn't defiance or a sign something's wrong; it reflects how young children's understanding of permanence develops. Expect the same questions to come up repeatedly.

**School-age children (roughly 6–12):** Children in this range generally understand that death is permanent and can experience grief more similarly to adults, including sadness, anger, and anxiety about other people they love dying too. They may also ask very direct, sometimes startling questions ("what happens to the body?") — these are usually genuine curiosity, not a cause for alarm.

**Teenagers:** Adolescents often grieve more like adults but may be less likely to bring it up unprompted, sometimes processing it more with peers than family, or appearing to cope by staying busy. Checking in periodically, without pressuring them to talk, tends to work better than waiting for them to initiate.

## Keep routines as steady as possible

Amid funeral planning and family upheaval, maintaining school schedules, mealtimes, and bedtimes gives children a sense of stability precisely when everything else feels uncertain. This doesn't mean shielding them from the loss — it means giving them a predictable structure to process it within.

## Let them be part of it, on their terms

Children often benefit from being included in family rituals — attending a funeral or memorial, if they want to and are prepared for what to expect beforehand, or contributing something like a drawing or a memory to share. Forcing participation isn't necessary or helpful, but excluding children entirely can leave them feeling shut out of something the rest of the family is going through together.

## Expect grief to look different than you'd expect

Children often grieve in bursts rather than continuously — deeply sad one moment, playing normally the next. This is developmentally typical, not a sign that they don't understand or don't care. Regression in younger children (bedwetting, clinginess, trouble sleeping) is also common and usually temporary.

## When to seek additional support

Most children move through grief with time, honesty, and steady support from the adults around them. It may be worth involving a child therapist or grief counselor if you notice, over an extended period:

- Persistent difficulty functioning at school or with friends
- Ongoing physical symptoms without a clear cause
- Expressions of guilt that the child somehow caused the death
- Signs the child is being asked to become the emotional caretaker of grieving adults, rather than the other way around

Many communities have grief support groups specifically for children, often run through hospice organizations or children's hospitals, which can be a valuable complement to family support.

## Taking care of yourself matters too

Children are often more affected by how the adults around them are coping than by the loss itself. Getting support for your own grief — from friends, family, or a counselor — isn't separate from supporting your children through theirs; it's part of it.

## Frequently asked questions

**Should I bring my child to the funeral?**
Generally yes, if the child wants to go and understands beforehand what to expect — but it should be their choice where possible, not a requirement.

**Is it normal for my child to seem unaffected?**
Yes — children often grieve in short bursts rather than continuously, and appearing to move on quickly doesn't mean the loss hasn't registered.

**How do I answer questions I don't have answers to, like what happens after death?**
It's okay to say you don't know, and to share what you personally believe if that feels right for your family — children generally respond better to honest uncertainty than to answers that feel evasive.
