# How to Manage a Loved One's Digital Accounts and Online Assets After Death

A generation ago, "settling an estate" meant paperwork, keys, and physical property. Today it also means email accounts, cloud photo libraries, cryptocurrency wallets, and payment apps — and most of these don't come with a clear instruction manual for what to do when the account holder has died. Here's a practical breakdown by account type.

## Why this category is different from the rest of the checklist

Most tasks after a death — closing a bank account, canceling a subscription — follow an established process, because financial institutions deal with deaths constantly. Digital accounts are newer territory: policies vary widely between companies, and some accounts (particularly cryptocurrency) may be genuinely unrecoverable without specific information the account holder had.

## Email and cloud storage

Email is often the master key to everything else — many other accounts use it for password resets, so it's worth prioritizing.

- **If you have the password**, you can typically log in directly, though check the provider's terms first — accessing someone else's account, even a deceased relative's, can technically violate some providers' terms of service without going through their official process.
- **If you don't have the password**, most major providers (Google, Microsoft, Apple, Yahoo) have a formal legacy or deceased-user process, usually requiring a death certificate and proof of your relationship or executor status. These processes exist specifically for this situation — see [Apple's Legacy Contact process](https://support.apple.com/en-us/102631) and [Google's Inactive Account Manager](https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/3036546) for the two most common examples.
- **Cloud storage and photos** (Google Photos, iCloud, Dropbox) are often tied to the same account as email, so resolving email access frequently resolves this too.

## Cryptocurrency

This is the account type most likely to be permanently lost, and it's worth understanding why before you start searching.

- Cryptocurrency held in a **personal wallet** (not an exchange) is typically accessible only with the private key or seed phrase. If the deceased didn't record this somewhere and share it, the funds may be genuinely unrecoverable — there's no customer service line to call, since it's not held by any company.
- Cryptocurrency held on an **exchange** (like Coinbase) works more like a traditional financial account — the exchange typically has a documented process for next of kin, similar to a bank.
- If you're searching for crypto holdings you suspect exist but can't locate, check for hardware wallets (small USB-like devices), written seed phrases in a safe or safe deposit box, and password manager entries.

## Payment apps with a balance

PayPal, Venmo, Cash App, and similar apps often hold real money and have their own next-of-kin or estate claim processes, generally requiring a death certificate. Check each app's support documentation directly, since specific requirements vary and change over time.

## Social media accounts

Most major platforms offer two options: **memorialization** (the account remains visible, often with a note indicating the person has passed, and can't be logged into) or **deletion**, upon request with proof of death. Which is appropriate is a personal and family decision — some families find a memorialized account meaningful, others find it painful to encounter.

## Domain names and monetized content

If the deceased owned a website domain, ran a blog, or had a monetized YouTube channel or similar, these have real (sometimes significant) value and their own transfer processes through the registrar or platform — this is easy to overlook amid everything else, but worth including on your list if it applies.

## A practical starting point

1. **Make a list of accounts** you know or suspect existed — check the person's email inbox for account confirmations and password reset emails, which often reveal accounts you didn't know about
2. **Check for a password manager** — if the deceased used one, it may be the single fastest way to access everything at once
3. **Prioritize accounts holding money** (crypto, payment apps, brokerage) over accounts holding sentimental value (photos, social media) if you're working with limited time or energy
4. **Keep a death certificate on hand** — nearly every formal digital-asset process requires one

This is just one category of a larger checklist. For everything else that needs handling after a death, see [our complete first-30-days guide](/blog/what-to-do-after-someone-dies-checklist).

## Frequently asked questions

**What happens to someone's email if no one has the password?**
Major providers have a formal process for next of kin or an executor to request access or account closure, typically requiring a death certificate. Response times and exact requirements vary by provider.

**Can I access a deceased person's cryptocurrency without their password?**
Only if it's held on an exchange, which has its own account-recovery process. Cryptocurrency in a personal, self-custodied wallet generally cannot be recovered without the private key or seed phrase — there's no company to contact.

**Should I memorialize or delete a deceased person's social media account?**
There's no universally right answer — it's a personal decision, and different family members may feel differently about it. Most platforms let you choose either option with proof of death.
